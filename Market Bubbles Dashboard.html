What "correlation to gold, rates, and bonds" actually means here

These four episodes sit under three completely different monetary regimes, and collapsing them into one gold line would mislead more than it informs:

EpisodeMonetary regimeWhat gold could do
1929Gold standard (Gold Standard Act, 1900)Fixed by law at $20.67/oz until 1933 — no market price exists to correlate. Revalued by statute to $35/oz in Jan 1934.
2000Fully floating, post-Bretton WoodsFree-floating; sat near a 20-year low as capital chased equities — an inverse relationship driven by opportunity cost, not crisis.
2008Fully floatingRose for most of the cycle as an inflation/dollar hedge, then sold off sharply in the Oct–Nov 2008 panic as leveraged funds liquidated every liquid asset to raise cash — a "correlations go to 1" liquidity effect, not a reversal of the hedge thesis.
2026Fully floatingNear all-time highs (~$4,098/oz) at the same time equities are at record valuations — an "everything rally" pattern, not the classic pre-panic flight-to-gold seen late in 2008.

1927–1932: The Terminal Boom and Crash

HOW TO READ THIS: The Fed cut rates in 1927, partly to help Britain defend its return to the gold standard — the conventional "start" of the blow-off phase, though historians dispute how directly that move caused the 1929 crash. The Dow peaked in September 1929 and didn't reclaim that level for 25 years.

Stages (Minsky/Kindleberger framework)

Displacement
1922–1927

NY Fed's Benjamin Strong shifts to open-market operations; Aug 1927 rate cut eases credit partly to support Britain's gold standard return.

Boom
1927–mid 1929

Real fundamentals genuinely strong — industrial production, freight, factory payrolls all hit records. Dow ~18%/yr compound 1922–29.

Euphoria
Mid-1929–Sept 3

Investment-trust IPOs peak; utility-holding-company pyramiding at its most extreme. Dow closes at cycle high 381.17.

Distress
Sept 5–Oct 23, 1929

Babson Break → Hatry fraud (London) → BoE hikes → Snowden's "orgy of speculation" remark → adverse utility-regulation rulings.

Panic
Oct 24–29, 1929

Black Thursday, Monday, Tuesday. A private bankers' pool intervenes on Thursday; fails to hold by Monday.

Revulsion
Nov 1929–Jul 1932

False Apr 1930 recovery, then bank panics (Nov 1930, Jun 1931) turn a recession into the Depression. Bottom: Jul 1932, −89.2%.

Sample-size caveat: this is one episode. Economic historians (Bierman vs. the popular "speculative orgy" narrative) dispute the causal chain even within this single case.

Cross-asset behaviour

Gold is not a market price here. The US was on the gold standard: gold was fixed at $20.67/oz by law until 1933, revalued to $35.00/oz by the Gold Reserve Act (30 Jan 1934). The chart below shows this as a flat administrative line, not a market signal — don't read anything into its "correlation" with the crash.
HOW TO READ THIS CHART: DJIA (left axis, log scale) shows the boom and the −89% collapse. The NY Fed discount rate rose into the peak (defending against speculation) and gold sat flat by law. Long-term government bond yields barely moved (3.3%–3.8%) through the entire cycle — a sign this was an equity-specific bubble, not a bond-market one. Warning signs present at the time

  • Investment-trust (closed-end fund) mania. $1bn+ raised in Aug–Sept 1929 alone vs $400m for all of 1928, often at ~50% premiums to net asset value.
  • Utility holding-company pyramiding. Layered financing structures left as little as $12,800–$38,400 of investor equity supporting $1,000,000 of underlying assets — effective leverage of 26–78x.
  • Margin lending. Brokers' loans exceeded $8.5bn by August 1929 — more than all US currency in circulation. Standard margin requirement: 50% (up to 75% on some stocks).
  • Valuation extremes — two measures, don't blend them. Current-earnings P/E for 45 industrials ran 10–15x (not extreme by Bierman's read); Shiller's CAPE-style measure hit 32.6x in Sept 1929 (extreme by any modern read).

1995–2002: The Dot-Com Bubble

HOW TO READ THIS: This is the cleanest "IPO mania" precedent of the three historical episodes — the one to hold up against 2026's AI-IPO wave. Watch the first-day pop statistics; they're the sharpest single number in this episode.

Stages

Displacement
1993–1995

Mosaic/web browser commercialization; falling rates; capital-gains relief; the Netscape IPO legitimizes pre-revenue listings.

Boom
1995–1998

Nasdaq crosses 1,000; VC capital pours in; Greenspan flags "irrational exuberance" but takes no action; 1998 LTCM-crisis rate cuts add fresh liquidity.

Euphoria

Distress

Panic / Revulsion

Cascading failures (Pets.com, Webvan); Fed cuts 6.5%→1.75%; 9/11; Enron/WorldCom/Adelphia scandals. Bottom Oct 2002.

HOW TO READ THIS CHART: Nasdaq's blow-off and collapse dwarfs every other series. Note gold sat near a 20-year low ($254/oz intra-year low, Jul 1999) almost exactly as the bubble peaked — capital rotated out of the "monetary metal" into speculative growth stocks. Warning signs present at the time

  • IPO first-day pops. 1999: 476 IPOs, mean first-day return 71.2% (86.7% for tech IPOs specifically); 114 tech IPOs doubled on debut. Compare to a 14.8% average 1990–98.
  • Profitless companies going public. 76% of 1999 IPOs and 81% of 2000 IPOs had negative trailing earnings, vs ~30% in 1990–98.
  • Price-to-sales multiples. Median tech-IPO P/S jumped to 26.5x (1999) and 31.7x (2000), from a 1995–98 average of ~3–8x.
  • Cosmetic renaming and stock splits. Adding ".com" to a company name produced a statistically significant stock-price pop (Cooper, Dimitrov & Rau, Journal of Finance, 2001) — pure signaling, no fundamental change.
  • Insider selling. Telecom/tech executives realized very large sums before the crash (Qwest, Global Crossing execs) — figures widely reported but not independently re-verified against SEC filings.
  • Infrastructure overbuild funded by debt. $500bn+ invested in fiber/telecom capacity after the 1996 Telecom Act, vastly outstripping demand — a direct analogue to today's AI data-center buildout debate.

2003–2009: The Housing & Credit Bubble

HOW TO READ THIS: This episode has a distinguishing feature versus 1929 and 2000: an 18–24 month "distress" phase (Aug 2006 – Aug 2008) preceded the acute panic. Watch the credit-spread chart, not the equity chart, for the earliest warnings.

Post-dot-com/9-11 Fed cuts rates 13 times to 1%; a "global pool of money" seeks yield above Treasuries.

Boom

Distress

Panic

Compressed into six weeks: Fannie/Freddie → Lehman → AIG → money-market freeze → TARP → TED spread peak.

Warning signs present at the time

  • Investment-bank leverage. FCIC: the five major investment banks ran leverage as high as 40:1 by 2007. Lehman: 24:1 (2003) → 31:1 (2007). Bear Stearns: 35.6:1 at end of FY2007.
  • Subprime lending growth & collapsing standards. Subprime share of originations: 5% ($35bn, 1994) → 20% ($600bn, 2006). Progression from "stated income" to "no income, no assets" (NINJA) loans.
  • Rating-agency AAA ratings on subprime tranches. FCIC named flawed models, issuer-paid ratings, and a "relentless drive for market share" as core causes.
  • CDS notional growth. Credit default swaps let investors make "multiple bets on the same securities"; AIG sold protection without adequate reserves and required >$180bn in government support.
  • Housing price-to-income ratio. 2.9–3.1x (1980–2001 average) → 4.6x (2006).

AI capex & a live natural experiment

The week of Jul 29–30, 2026 earnings split sharply: Microsoft +15.5% in one day (~$450bn market-cap gain, the largest one-day gain for any US company on record) on strong Azure/AI numbers, while Meta fell 7.95% (an 11-day losing streak, its first earnings miss in 3 years) as AI data-center lease obligations swelled to $279bn. Oracle's market cap has fallen ~50–58% from its Sept 2025 peak on fears it will need up to $500bn in fresh capital for its AI buildout. Private valuations are moving even faster and with no liquidity event to test them: Anthropic went from $380bn (Feb 2026) to $965bn (May 2026) — nearly tripling in three months.

A realized blow-up, not just a ratio: the "Situational Awareness" AI-focused hedge fund collapsed from $45bn to ~$10bn AUM in one month (late Jul 2026) on concentrated leveraged AI bets, forcing a Citadel-brokered rescue with Goldman/BofA/JPMorgan as prime brokers — financial press dubbed it "Archegos 2.0." (FT, Bloomberg, CNBC, ZeroHedge.)

The 2026 bull case and bear case

Bear case: this rhymes

  • Valuation is at a historical extreme. CAPE (40.6) exceeds both the 1929 and 2008 peaks and is closing in on the all-time 1999 high; the Buffett Indicator hit an all-time high in May 2026; S&P 500 Price/Sales exceeds its own recorded historical maximum.
  • Concentration is worse than 2000. TMT is 49% of the S&P 500 — nine points above the dot-com peak. Passive flows mechanically concentrate capital into a handful of names.
  • Leverage is building fast, off-index. FINRA margin debt hit a third consecutive monthly record in June 2026 (+51.5% YoY) — a metric that has historically preceded market tops by 2–4 months in the 2000, 2007, and 2021 cycles.
  • Private markups with no liquidity event. Anthropic's valuation nearly tripled in three months with no public listing to test it — a recognized late-cycle pattern.
  • A real blow-up has already happened. The Situational Awareness hedge fund's collapse is the first concrete, leverage-driven, AI-concentrated unwind of this cycle, with major-bank prime-broker exposure echoing Archegos (2021).
  • Circular financing is proliferating. Nvidia backstopping its own customers' data-center debt, Google guaranteeing a competitor-adjacent project — arrangements that inflate reported demand without new end-customer revenue.

Bull case: this diverges

  • No euphoria at the retail level. AAII bullish sentiment sits below its long-term average and CNN's Fear & Greed Index reads "Fear," down sharply from "Greed" a year ago — the opposite of the blow-off-top psychology seen in 1929 and 2000.
  • Breadth is improving, not narrowing. The equal-weight S&P 500 outperformed the cap-weighted index through H1 2026 — a genuine broadening, unlike the narrowing that historically preceded the 2000 top.
  • The yield curve is normal. None of the three historical bubbles popped with a normal curve; all three saw inversion well before their crashes.
  • The market is discriminating, not indiscriminate. Microsoft and Alphabet are rewarded for monetizing AI capex; Meta and Oracle are punished for capex that isn't yet paying off — a selectivity absent from a classic undiscriminating blow-off top.
  • Today's rates are higher, not lower. The 10-year sits near 4.7%, well above the ~1% environment that historically justifies stretched multiples — today's valuations are stretched despite higher discount rates, a different mechanism from 2000's low-rate-driven multiple expansion.
  • Credible bull and neutral voices argue this capex is real. Unlike 1999–2000's largely pre-revenue speculation, several analysts (Yardeni, UBS, T. Rowe Price, Raymond James) tie current AI spend to identifiable, monetizing business use.

Both cases draw on the same dataset in the 2026 tab above. Neither is a prediction — read them as the strongest good-faith version of each argument, then weigh the evidence yourself.
